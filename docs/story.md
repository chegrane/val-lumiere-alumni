# The story

**Goal of this file:** learn how we go from a human need to a well-defined project.

> **Fictional institution.** Université de Val-Lumière, its staff and its graduates do not exist. Every name here is invented.

The story is told in the order things happen: first a request, then meetings with the people who do the work, then the people the information is about, and at the end, an agreement on what to build. The story says **what** the university needs; **how** we represent it comes next, in [model.md](model.md).

---

## 1. The request (week 1)

The Director of the Alumni Relations Office sends us this message:

> Université de Val-Lumière, a public university in Québec, has awarded degrees to about 60,000 people since it was founded. Our office exists to keep a lasting link with them: we send them news, we invite them to our events, and we call on them when the university needs their experience.
>
> Our information is spread over many files, and it is getting harder every year to know who our graduates are and how to reach them. Recently we made a painful mistake because of it.
>
> We need your help to find a better way of working.

That is all we have at the start: a goal, a feeling that things are not working, and no detailed account yet of the problems or of what is needed.

---

## 2. Meeting the office (weeks 2 and 3)

Before proposing anything, we meet the people who do the work every day. For each one: what they do, what goes wrong, and the answers they give to our questions.

### The newsletter manager

**What she does:** sends a newsletter every month to a mailing list of graduates. The emails are sent with the university's email service (Office 365).

**What goes wrong:**
- "About a third of our emails bounce back. I don't know which ones are dead, so I keep sending to them."
- "Some people ask us to stop all contact, but I only manage the newsletter list. The events team keeps its own list, so these people may still receive invitations."
- "When someone calls to say *stop writing to me*, I write it on a sticky note. Sometimes I'm not the one who answers the phone."

**Her answers:**
- *Which email do you use?* "Graduates keep their university address, but many stop reading it. They give us a personal or a work address instead. One person can have several emails and phones; we should use the one they prefer."
- *Who sends?* "Office 365 sends, and it will keep sending. What I need is the right list of people: who may receive this message, at which address."
- *What does "reachable" mean?* "An address is good until a message to it bounces; Office 365 tells us which ones bounced. An address is *checked* when the person confirms it, or when a message to it was delivered."

### The events coordinator

**What he does:** organises the annual reunion and smaller events, for the whole university or for one department. Every year he builds a new invitation list by copying the faculties' spreadsheets into one.

**What goes wrong:**
- "The same person shows up two or three times: *Marie-Ève Tremblay*, *Marie Eve Trembley*, *M. Tremblay*. Same person? I guess, I check by hand."
- "Last year we sent a reunion invitation to a graduate who had passed away. His family called us. They were hurt. That must never happen again."
- "I know who registered, more or less. Who actually came? Paper lists at the door. I never enter them anywhere."
- "For the reunion I get help from student volunteers. I give them the whole spreadsheet, home addresses included. I don't like it, but I don't have anything else."

**His answers:**
- *Whom do you invite?* "It depends. The math department invites its math graduates. For the reunion, it's everyone, and someone with two degrees must get **one** invitation, not two."
- *How do you learn that a graduate has died?* "Usually the family calls us, or a letter comes back with a note, sometimes we see an obituary. We should note it, with the date and how we learned it, and from then on nobody sends that person anything."

### A faculty secretary (Faculty of Engineering)

**What she does:** keeps her faculty's spreadsheet of graduates: name, program, year. Professors also ask her for graduates who could give a talk in a course, mentor students, sit on a jury or help with grading. These are small paid assignments.

**What goes wrong:**
- "Some graduates did a bachelor's here, then a master's years later, sometimes in another faculty. They are in two spreadsheets, and they receive everything twice."
- "Some changed their name since they graduated. Their diploma is under the old name; their emails come with the new one. I can't always connect them."
- "I never know when an address was last checked. Some lines are twenty years old."
- "For assignments, I search my memory and my old emails to find someone."

**Her answers:**
- *How do you know two lines are the same person?* "Every student receives a student number from the registrar at admission, and keeps it for life, even after graduation. When we have it, it's easy. But our old spreadsheets, from the 1990s, often don't have it, or have it typed wrong. Then we compare names, years and programs, and a person in the office decides."
- *Who handles applications and pay for assignments?* "Human Resources, in their own system. What I need is to send the offer to the right graduates: those who agreed to receive offers, in the right field. After that, they apply with HR."

### The registrar's office

**What they do:** keep the official academic records of every student: student number, program, courses, grades, degrees, and the name at the time of graduation.

**Their answers:**
- "These records are official. The law requires us to keep them, in our own system. The alumni office does not change them."
- "We can give the alumni office a copy of each graduate's degrees: student number, date of birth, program, faculty, year, name at graduation. After the first copy, we send only the new degrees, once a year."
- "If a graduate changes name later, the diploma keeps the original name. The alumni office can use the current name in its own work."

### The privacy officer

Under Québec's law, the head of the university (the rector) is responsible for the protection of personal information, and may delegate this role in writing. At Val-Lumière, the rector delegated it to her.

**What she explains:**
- "We may use personal information only for the purposes the person agreed to. Someone can agree to the newsletter and refuse everything else."
- "Anyone can ask us in writing: *what do you know about me?* We must answer completely, within the legal deadline. They can also ask us to correct what is wrong or incomplete."
- "Deleting is not automatic. Some records must be kept by law, and others must be destroyed once we no longer need them. Each request is checked."
- "Two requests are often confused. *Stop contacting me* is not the same as *remove my information*. The first one we must respect right away; the second one we must examine."
- "Today, nobody knows who asked not to be contacted, for what, or since when. People asked by phone, by email; it was noted in one file and not in the others. We have contacted people who had asked us to stop."
- "Not everyone in the office needs to see everything. A student volunteer does not need home addresses. Someone preparing statistics does not need names. Former names are seen only by the staff who need them."
- "Last year someone asked to be deleted. We removed their lines from a few files, and the next report showed fewer graduates for 2010 than the year before. Totals must not change because someone left."

**Her answers:**
- *What can be removed, then?* "The academic records stay with the registrar. What the alumni office holds — contact information, preferences, event history — can be removed when a person asks and nothing requires us to keep it. Their degrees still count in the totals, without their name or anything that directly identifies them. We should not call that *anonymous*: in a very small program, a program and a year can still point to one person."
- *Then they are gone for good?* "Not completely: we keep the minimum, their student number on a do-not-contact list, with the reason and since when. Otherwise they come back with the next spreadsheet or the next copy from the registrar. A person who asks for removal is never contacted again. We also keep a note that the removal was done, and when, without saying for whom."

### The Director

At the end of the meetings, we ask the Director one question.

- *What happens to the old spreadsheets and mailing lists?* "We bring them into the new tool once. Then they are archived: we can still read them if needed, but nobody updates them anymore. The new tool is the only one we use."

## 3. Asking the graduates (week 3)

The office talks *about* graduates all the time. We also need to hear *from* them. With the office's help, we send a short questionnaire to a group of volunteer graduates, and we interview five of them.

| Graduate | Their situation | What they told us |
|---|---|---|
| **Léa**, computer science 2024 | No longer reads her university email | "I'd like news and job events, at my personal email only. Just let me update my address easily." |
| **Sophie**, education 2009 | Graduated as *Sophie Gagnon*, now *Sophie Roy* | "Please use my current name. But yes, my diploma is under my old one; I understand you need both." |
| **Robert**, administration 1995 | Asked twice by phone to stop all contact | "And I still got an invitation. I want it respected everywhere. And I'd like to know what you still have about me." |
| **Karim**, bachelor 1998 (science), master 2012 (engineering) | Appears in two faculties | "I get every message twice. It looks like you don't know me." |
| **Nadia**, civil engineering 2003, 20 years in industry | Would like to give back | "I'd gladly give talks or mentor students. But I don't want the newsletter, and please send me offers only in my field." |

**What these conversations tell us** — and they confirm what the staff described:
- Graduates want to **choose what they receive and where** (which email, no phone), separately for each kind of message.
- They want to **update their own information** easily, without a website for now: an email to the office must be enough.
- They want to **be recognised as one person**, even with two degrees or a new name.
- They want their **"no" to be respected everywhere**, and to **know what is kept** about them.

Five interviews are a starting point, not the opinion of 60,000 graduates, but they point in the same direction as the staff.

## 4. What we agreed to build (week 4)

From these meetings, we wrote a summary. The Director read it, corrected it and approved it.

**One reliable place, shared by the whole office, to:**

1. **Keep in touch** — know who our graduates are, what they studied, in which faculty, in which year, and how to reach them today.
2. **Organise the annual reunion and other events** — prepare the list of people to invite, record who registered, and record who actually came.
3. **Send assignment offers to the right graduates** — those who agreed to receive offers, in the right field. Applications, selection and pay stay with Human Resources.
4. **Respect each person's wishes** — for each kind of contact separately: newsletter, events, assignment offers. Someone may ask us to stop contacting them, or ask us to remove their information.
5. **Trust our information** — know how good it is, and see it improve month after month.

**How it will work:**
- **One person, one entry.** The registrar's student number links a person to their degrees. Old entries without a student number are compared by name, year and program; a staff member confirms each match.
- **People and degrees are both counted.** A department event targets degrees (its own graduates); a university event targets people, one invitation each.
- **Degrees come from the registrar.** The alumni office does not change them; it keeps its own current name, contacts and preferences for each person.
- **Several contacts per person.** Each email or phone has its kind (university, personal, work), whether it still works, when it was last checked, and one is marked as preferred.
- **The tool prepares the lists; Office 365 sends.** Bounced emails come back into the tool and mark the address as not working.
- **A death closes all contact.** We record it with the date and how we learned it.
- **The old spreadsheets and lists are imported once, then archived** as read-only. Only the new tool is used.

**Who uses it:**
- The office staff: newsletter, events, faculty secretaries, the privacy officer.
- Student volunteers, for events only, with limited access.
- The person preparing statistics, without names.

**Who does not use it (for now): the graduates themselves.** When a graduate asks to see or correct their information, to stop being contacted, or to have their information removed, they write to the office, and **the staff use the tool to answer completely**.

## 5. Rules and limits (week 4)

**What we must respect**
- **The law.** Collect, use, share and keep personal information only as the law allows, and ask for the person's consent wherever it is required (for example, for the newsletter or for assignment offers). Tell a person everything we hold about them, within the legal deadline, and correct what is wrong or incomplete. Examine each removal request against what the law requires us to keep or to destroy.
- **"Stop" is immediate.** This is the office's own rule: a request to stop contacting someone is respected right away, everywhere. We keep who asked, for what, since when and how, so that it stays respected when the person reappears in an imported spreadsheet.
- **Confidentiality.** Each person in the office sees only what their work needs. Former names are seen only by the staff who need them.
- **Removing without breaking history.** When a person asks us to remove their information, the alumni office's information about them goes (contacts, preferences, event history); their degrees still count in the totals, without their name or anything that directly identifies them. We keep only their student number on a do-not-contact list, so they are never brought back or contacted again, and a note that the removal was done, and when.

*The law is simplified here for a fictional story; this is not legal advice.*

**What we do not build now**
- No website or mobile application for the graduates.
- No donations or fundraising.
- No sending of emails: Office 365 sends.
- No applications, selection or pay for assignments: Human Resources handles them.
- No social network, chat feature or built-in messaging platform.

These may come later. Today, we want a solid foundation.

## 6. How we will know it works

Today, each of these questions takes days, or cannot be answered at all. With the new tool, the answers must be right, and fast: about a minute for the questions about our information (1 to 7, and 10). Questions 8 and 9 are requests from a person: they must be handled completely, with a trace, within the legal deadline and our own shorter target.

1. How many graduates do we have — people and degrees — by faculty and by year?
2. How many of them can we actually reach today?
3. Whom can we invite to this year's reunion: reachable, alive, and who agreed to receive invitations — one invitation per person?
4. Which engineering graduates agreed to receive assignment offers?
5. When was this person's contact information last checked?
6. Who asked not to be contacted, for what, and since when?
7. How many people appear more than once in our records, and which ones?
8. A graduate asks: "What do you know about me?" Can we answer completely?
9. A graduate asks us to remove their information. Can we do what is allowed, and prove it, without changing our statistics?
10. Month after month: is our information getting better or worse?
